ShotlyISO 400·36 EXP·PRODUCT DESIGN

You get 15 shots. No do-overs. They develop in the morning.

A product and experience design for a wedding camera that lives on the guests' phones — plus an honest read on which parts of the idea will work at a real wedding and which parts won't.

The photographer captures the wedding. The guests capture everything else. הצלם מצלם את החתונה. האורחים מצלמים את כל השאר.

The read

The concept is strong, and its strength is not the album — it's the constraint. Every competitor in this space sells unlimited uploads, and every one of them produces the same thing: two thousand near-identical photos nobody opens twice. Fifteen shots produces a different artifact. A guest with fifteen shots looks around the room before pressing the button.

But the brief as written contains three contradictions that will break the product at a real wedding, and I've resolved all three rather than shipping around them. No-Preview cannot coexist with live reactions — if nothing is visible tonight, there is nothing to react to tonight. A live leaderboard is a liability at an event with grandparents, exes, and a rabbi. And a mobile web app has no push notifications, which means the entire live-game layer has no way to reach a guest whose phone is in their pocket — unless the venue screen becomes the notification channel, which reclassifies it from an upsell to core infrastructure.

Two questions worth holding onto. Would a guest actually open this at a wedding? Only if the shutter feels better than their own camera app and something they can't get elsewhere is waiting on the other side. Would the couple feel it gave them something the photographer couldn't? Yes — but not from the photos. From the voice notes, the secret memories, and the ninety seconds at 01:40 that nobody was supposed to see.

01

What the product actually is

Structure

Three surfaces, one event. Everything else is a setting on one of them.

Surface 01 · the product

The Guest Camera

Mobile web, no install, no account. A viewfinder, a frame counter, a shutter. Reached by scanning a card on the table. This is 95% of all usage and 100% of the content. If this screen isn't excellent, nothing else matters.

Surface 02 · the purchase

The Couple's Studio

Two different products wearing one login. Before the wedding it's a setup flow that should feel like loading a camera. After the wedding it's a memory experience that should contain no settings at all.

Surface 03 · the megaphone

The Venue Screen

A projector page at the hall. Your only broadcast channel — mobile web can't push. Every live mechanic in the product depends on this screen existing, so it cannot be a premium-tier extra.

The object nobody counts as a surface

There is a fourth: the Camera Pass, the printed card on every table. It's the only part of the product a guest sees before deciding whether to participate, the only part other wedding suppliers ever see, and the cheapest brand asset you will ever own. It deserves as much design attention as the camera screen. More on it in 08.

One structural rule: the original file is sacred. The wedding's visual style is a presentation layer — applied on display, baked only at export, never written into storage. This costs a little compute and buys three things: the couple can re-develop the whole wedding in a different style next week, a bad style choice made three months before the wedding isn't permanent, and "try your wedding in black & white" becomes a delightful moment instead of a support ticket.

02

The couple, in three acts

Journey

Act I — Loading the camera (3 months out to the morning of)

The failure mode here is obvious: eighteen toggles on one settings page and a couple who abandons at step four. The fix is to make setup a sequence of small pleasures, each producing something they can see.

Step 1

Names and date

Nothing else. The event exists after twenty seconds, and every later screen is decorated with their names — which makes it feel like theirs immediately.

Step 2

Choose your camera

The style picker, framed as choosing the physical camera their guests will hold — not as picking a filter. Each style is a camera body with a name, a film stock, and a live sample photo that visibly changes. This is the emotional peak of setup; it should be the most beautiful screen in the studio.

Step 3

How much film

10 / 15 / 24 / 36 exposures. Default 15. Label them in film language — 36 EXP is a real roll, and couples who recognise it will feel the product knows what it's talking about.

Step 4

When it develops

Camera mode (see 05) plus reveal time. Default: Darkroom mode, reveal at 10:00 the next morning. Show a preview of the guest's screen for each option so the choice is visual, not verbal.

Step 5

The night's shape

Ceremony, dinner, dancing. Three time pickers. This one input drives timed challenges, Ceremony Mode, the venue screen's rhythm, and the recap's chapter breaks. It's the highest-leverage field in the product and couples will happily fill it in.

Step 6

Pick your challenge packs

Packs, not a checklist of forty items. Classic, Chaos, Family, Dancefloor, Israeli Wedding — each expands so they can delete the two they hate and write one of their own. Editing beats authoring.

Step 7

Print the passes

Generates a print-ready PDF: table cards, an entrance poster, bar cards, and the bonus-film cards for hidden locations. Also generates a one-page MC script — thirty seconds of what to say and when. Send this to the DJ.

Free to build, pay to publish. Let couples complete the entire setup, see their Camera Pass, and preview the guest camera for free. Ask for money only when they hit "activate my wedding". They'll have already fallen in love with it, and you get their contact details either way.

Act II — The night (they should barely touch it)

A couple on their wedding day has approximately zero attention available. Their live view should be a single glanceable page: how many guests have joined, how many shots taken, and one button — launch a live mission. Everything else, including moderation, is delegated.

Best Man Mode is the answer to moderation. The couple assigns one or two friends as moderators during setup; those people get a link, and reported or auto-flagged photos go to them. The bride is not reviewing photos during her own wedding. Small feature, very large trust payoff, and exactly the kind of detail that gets a product recommended by wedding planners.

Act III — The morning after, and the year after

This is a completely different product and it should look like one: no settings, no counts of "uploads", no admin vocabulary. The post-wedding studio has one job — make two people lose ninety minutes. Sections, in the order they should appear: Your night (the assembled story), The Awards, Secret memories, Voice notes, Every frame, Your guests, Take it all.

Put secret memories and voice notes above the full gallery. The gallery is the commodity; the private things are what the photographer could not have given them. Leading with the gallery wastes the strongest card in the deck.

03

The guest, from scan to next morning

Journey

The whole design constraint: a guest is holding a drink, standing up, in low light, slightly drunk, and will give you about eight seconds before deciding this isn't worth it.

0:00

The scan

They read the card because it doesn't say "upload your photos here". It says יש לך 15 צילומים. אל תבזבז אותם. The camera opens instantly — no splash, no logo animation, no "welcome to". The viewfinder is already live behind the first screen.

0:08

Who are you

First name. That's the entire required form. Then one optional tap: which table? — presented as a grid of numbers, not a text field. Table is worth asking for because it powers table albums, table awards, and a printable table photo book later, all without touching biometrics.

0:20

First frame

They take a photo. Flash fires, shutter sounds, phone buzzes, the counter ticks 15 → 14 with a mechanical film-advance motion. No preview appears. Just: פריים 1 נשמר. The whole interaction is under 400ms and it must feel better than their native camera app, or the product is dead.

0:35

The ask, at the right moment

Now — after the first shot, when they're invested, and not before — a single line: לאן לשלוח לך את התמונות מחר? Phone or email. Skippable. This solves reveal delivery, device loss, and hands you a list of ~150 wedding-age contacts per event. See 08.

22:15

Something develops

Their first frames finish developing and appear in the Darkroom alongside everyone else's. This is the moment that decides whether they come back. It must feel like a discovery, not a notification.

23:40

The mission

The MC says the line, the projector turns red, and every phone in the room with the tab open flips to a takeover: 3 minutes. The best dancefloor photo of the night. This is the peak of the evening's game layer, and it happens once or twice — not every twenty minutes.

01:20

Last frame

They shoot their final exposure. The counter reads 00. Instead of a dead end, the roll ends with a question: רוצה להשאיר להם משהו? — a 15-second voice note. This is the single best-placed prompt in the product. The moment their roll ends is the moment they're most sentimental, and voice notes are the thing the couple will cry over.

10:00

Developed

A message arrives. Not "your album is ready" — החתונה של מייה ודניאל פותחה 🎮. They open it hungover in bed, see the whole night, and then see the line that brings them back a third time: we found 32 photos of you.

The hardest number in this product is the scan rate. Everything downstream is multiplied by it. Realistic target for a 350-guest Israeli wedding: 35–45% scan, and about 60% of those take more than three photos. That's ~130 photographers producing ~1,400 frames. Below 25% the Darkroom looks empty and the game layer collapses.

Four levers, in order of impact: the MC saying one sentence (worth more than everything else combined), the card design, bonus-film cards hidden around the venue, and seeding the Darkroom before guests arrive so it's never empty (see 06).

05

The game, rebuilt around film

Mechanics

The brief has two parallel economies — shots and points — and neither is doing much work. Collapse them into one.

Film is the currency

Points are abstract. Film is the thing the guest already cares about, already sees on every screen, and already fears running out of. So every reward in the product pays out in film. Completing a challenge gives you exposures. Winning a mission gives you exposures. Your photo getting reactions gives you exposures.

This does three jobs at once. It removes the product's most predictable complaint — "I ran out and now it's useless" becomes "I need to complete something to get more". It makes challenges genuinely motivating without a leaderboard. And it gives the reward moment real texture: +2 EXP landing on the counter with a film-advance sound is a far better feeling than +40 pts.

ActionPayoutWhy this number
Starting roll15 EXPEnough to be generous, few enough to be deliberate.
Complete a challenge+2 EXPNet positive after the shot it costs — challenges should feel free.
Your photo passes 10 reactions+1 EXPRewards quality without requiring you to check.
Win a live mission+5 EXPLarge enough to chase, rare enough not to inflate.
Find a hidden Camera Pass+3 EXPThe physical/digital crossover. See below.
Leave a voice note+2 EXPPays guests to give the couple the most valuable content in the product.

Hidden film — the mechanic that moves people around the room

Print a handful of extra Camera Passes and hide them: behind the bar, on the smoking balcony, at the grandparents' table, backstage with the band. Scanning one gives +3 EXP and unlocks a location-specific challenge. It costs nothing, it turns the printed card from a one-time entry point into a collectible, and it physically distributes photographers into the corners of the wedding where the interesting things are actually happening — which is precisely the content the professional never gets.

Three camera modes, and why the middle one is the product

Mode A

Film Roll

Nothing is visible to anyone until the reveal. The purest expression of the idea, and the quietest. No reactions, no live screen content, no game. Right for small, elegant, older weddings.

Mode B · default · the signature

Darkroom

A photo becomes visible to everyone about 25 minutes after it's taken. No retakes, no instant gratification — but the night still unfolds live, always lagging, slightly dreamlike. Guests come back because something of theirs is developing.

Mode C

Instant

Everything visible immediately. Loses the magic, keeps the shot limit. Exists because some couples will insist, and because it's right for an engagement party or a bachelorette.

The develop delay is the invention here. It's the only design that satisfies every constraint at once: no retakes (the photo is gone the instant you press the button), a live social layer (something new is always appearing), a reason to reopen the tab (yours is developing), content for the venue screen, and a Darkroom that fills gradually all night instead of dumping at 10:00 the next morning.

Make the delay visible and anticipatory: a developing frame is a dark rectangle with a countdown and a faint chemical shimmer, not a grey skeleton. Guests should be able to see their own photos in the queue, developing, without seeing what's in them.

Challenges: packs, not lists

Two kinds. Standing challenges are open all night — a full-table photo, someone crying, the best-dressed guest, a selfie with someone you met tonight. Timed challenges unlock against the night's schedule the couple entered at setup: the first dance, the parents dancing, the last twenty minutes.

Ship packs of six to eight written by someone with taste, and let the couple delete and add. An Israeli Wedding pack should exist and should be written in Hebrew by someone who has been to one — מישהו על הכתפיים, ההורים רוקדים, מי בכה ראשון, השולחן שלא קם כל הערב. Localised humour is not a translation task and shouldn't be treated as one.

One rule that matters: a challenge never costs an extra shot. You shoot from your existing roll and get more back for completing it. Charging extra film for a challenge makes guests avoid them.

Live missions, done honestly

A three-minute mission with a voting round is a genuinely great party mechanic, and it will fail silently if nobody knows it started. So: MC- or couple-triggered only, never on a timer, always accompanied by the venue screen going red, and limited to two or three across the night. Structure: 3 minutes to shoot, 2 minutes of voting (submissions bypass the develop delay — the one exception), winner on the projector with their name. Winner gets +5 EXP and a share card.

Good mission types: best dancefloor photo, best group photo, worst photo of the night (deliberately — it produces the funniest submissions), best photo of someone who doesn't know you're taking it, best table.

The leaderboard question

Default it off. A visible ranking during a wedding turns a warm room into a competition and structurally excludes anyone over sixty. The same anticipation is available for free through awards revealed at the end — nobody sees the standings, everybody finds out who won at 01:45 on the projector. If a couple wants the leaderboard, give it to them, but as a Top 5 only, never a full ranking. Nobody needs to learn they're 47th at a wedding.

06

Getting guests to actually participate

Adoption

Every feature in this document is multiplied by the scan rate, and the scan rate is decided by things that mostly aren't software.

Highest leverage

One sentence from the MC

"There's a card on your table. You each have fifteen photos tonight — the couple sees them tomorrow morning." Thirty seconds from the person the whole room is already listening to. Ship a printed MC script in the setup PDF and make it impossible to miss. Worth more than every other adoption lever combined.

Cold start

Never show an empty Darkroom

The roll opens two hours before the ceremony for the wedding party — getting-ready shots, the parents arriving, the nervous ten minutes before. By the time the first guest scans, the Darkroom already holds forty photos and reads as a party in progress. It also captures a window the photographer usually bills extra for.

Second visit

Something is always developing

The develop timer is a built-in reason to reopen the tab. Reinforce it once, gently, at the moment their first frames land — a subtle in-tab change, never a permission prompt.

Third visit

Somebody reacted to yours

Show it in the Darkroom, not as a notification. "Three people loved your frame" on your own photo when they next open the tab is enough.

Physical

Passes everywhere, not just tables

Entrance poster, bar, bathroom mirrors, dancefloor edge, dessert table, smoking area. Each one is a fresh entry point for someone who wasn't sitting down when the MC spoke.

Design

A card that provokes

יש לך 15 צילומים. אל תבזבז אותם. — a challenge, not an instruction. "Capture what the photographer won't" as the second line. The card shouldn't explain the product; it should make scanning irresistible.

What to do about the guests who won't participate at all

Roughly half the room won't scan, and a meaningful fraction of those are older relatives holding the most emotionally valuable perspective in the building. Two cheap interventions: a Camera Pass at the grandparents' table with a bigger QR and larger type, and a mission built to bridge it — "get someone over 60 to take a photo of you" pays film to the young guest and quietly hands a phone to the person who was never going to scan.

07

Where the feeling lives

Emotion

Peak-end: people compress an experience into its most intense moment and its last one. This product has four peaks it can control precisely.

Peak 1

The shutter

The most important 400 milliseconds in the product. Screen flash, a real mechanical shutter sound, a haptic pulse, the counter physically advancing like a film wind, and the frame flying off toward the darkroom. It has to feel better than their native camera. If a guest presses it twice just because it's satisfying, everything downstream works.

Peak 2

Your frames developed

Around 22:15 the first of their own photos resolves. Do this as a physical develop animation — the image emerging from black through chemical warmth — not a fade-in. Three seconds, once per photo, and they'll watch every one.

Peak 3

Developed — the couple, 10:00

See 10. Built as a single directed sequence, not a page load.

Peak 4

"We found 32 photos of you"

The guest's own peak, the next day. Almost nobody in their life has ever handed them 32 photos of themselves having a good time. This is the most shareable moment in the product, and it belongs to the guests, not the couple.

The end

The last frame

Endings are remembered as much as peaks, and this product has a built-in one: running out of film. Don't waste it on an error state. זהו. הפילם נגמר. — the roll is finished, here's what you shot tonight, and would you like to leave them something? Voice note, and out.

The four things that will actually make them cry

  • Voice notes. A drunk friend at 01:00 saying something true. No photograph competes with this, and no professional package includes it. If one feature justifies the price, it's this one.
  • Secret memories. Content deliberately withheld from the room and given only to them. The secrecy is the emotional mechanism, not the content.
  • Being photographed by people who love you. A professional photograph is technically better and emotionally neutral. A slightly blurry photo taken by your sister from three metres away is not.
  • The parts they missed. A couple sees perhaps 30% of their own wedding. The photos from table 14 at 23:40 are, genuinely, new information about their own night.

Don't over-decorate the emotion. The strongest moments in this product are quiet — a voice, a bad photo, a sentence. Confetti animations and heavy copy will cheapen them. Save the motion for the game layer and let the memory layer be still.

08

How it spreads

Growth

Weddings are the best viral substrate in consumer software: 350 people, most of them the right age, all experiencing the product at once, in a category where people copy what they saw at the last one.

Loop 1 — guest becomes couple (the main one)

Every wedding puts the product in front of ~150 people who gave you a phone number, in a room full of people at the exact life stage where they might be next. Israeli weddings are dense with this: a group of friends gets married across two years and attends all of each other's weddings.

The mechanism isn't an ad. It's a single line at the bottom of a guest's personal album the next morning: "Getting married? Maya & Daniel get a credit if you use their camera." Both sides benefit, the couple has a reason to forward it, and the guest is asked at the one moment they feel most positive about weddings in general.

Loop 2 — the award card

Winning "Funniest Photo at Maya & Daniel's wedding" produces a share asset that is genuinely about the guest, not about you. Make it a stylised film print — their winning photo, the award, the couple's names, and the film edge rebate along the border. That edge marking is your logo, and it will be on Instagram at 02:00 while the wedding is still going.

Loop 3 — the film rebate (the quiet one)

Real film has text printed along the edge of the negative: stock, frame number, manufacturer. Print the same thing on every exported photo — SHOTLY 400 · MAYA & DANIEL · 23 — in tiny orange type down the edge. It's beautiful, it's authentic to the medium, guests won't crop it out because it looks intentional, and it's a watermark nobody experiences as a watermark. The highest-ROI branding decision in the product.

Loop 4 — the venue and the supplier chain

A venue hosts 120 weddings a year. A photographer shoots 40. A DJ works 60. All three see the Camera Pass on every table, and all three are asked for recommendations by every couple they meet. Give them a wholesale price and a referral code. This will outperform paid acquisition by a wide margin in the Israeli market, where the wedding supplier network is small and highly interconnected.

The photographer is the delicate one. Framed wrong, you're a competitor putting phones in the air. Framed right — Ceremony Mode protects their ceremony, the photographer lane lets them deliver sneak-peeks the same night, and the product explicitly says they capture the wedding — you become something they sell as an add-on at ₪1,200 having paid ₪500.

Loop 5 — the people who weren't there

Every wedding has fifty people who couldn't come — grandparents abroad, a friend with a newborn, family who couldn't travel. A "watch it from wherever you are" link, sent by the couple, is emotionally strong and drops the product in front of an entirely new set of people at zero cost.

09

Privacy, moderation, and the things that go wrong

Trust

A wedding contains children, exes, employers, religious relatives, and at least one person who should not be photographed at 01:30. Get this wrong once and it's the only thing anyone remembers.

The moderation stack

  • Automatic screening on every upload. Explicit-content classification before a photo can reach the Darkroom or the projector. Non-negotiable, cheap, and the only thing standing between you and an incident on a three-metre screen.
  • A hold buffer on the venue screen. The projector runs 60–90 seconds behind the Darkroom. That window is the difference between a moderator catching something and a room seeing it.
  • Best Man Mode. Moderation delegated to one or two friends, never the couple. Two buttons: keep, remove. Removal is instant and silent — the photographer of the removed frame is never told, because telling them starts an argument at a wedding.
  • Long-press to report on every photo. Two reports auto-hide pending review. Guests are the fastest moderators in the room.
  • Approval-before-public mode for couples who want it, off by default. It kills the live feeling and most couples won't need it, but conservative and religious weddings will, and it should be one toggle.

The consent question, answered properly

Photographing people at a private event you were invited to is normal and legal; the risk is in what the product does with the photos afterwards. Three concrete positions:

  • The event is private by default and stays private. Unlisted link, no public URLs, no discovery, no indexing, no cross-wedding anything. A photo taken at one wedding never appears anywhere else.
  • Face grouping is opt-in per guest and per wedding. When personal albums ship: the guest opts in by taking one selfie, the face index is scoped to that single event, it's deleted with the event, and it's never used to identify anyone across weddings. Under Israeli privacy law and GDPR this is biometric data — treat it that way, get explicit consent, don't default it on. The non-biometric version ships first: your own shots, your table's shots, and photos other guests tag you in. That covers most of the emotional payload with none of the exposure.
  • Anyone can remove themselves. A visible "remove this photo of me" on every frame, routing to the moderator, plus a guest-side "delete everything I took" that actually deletes. This is a legal requirement and also just correct.

Secret memories need a rule

A photo marked רק לזוג is invisible to every guest, to the venue screen, to the awards, and to the moderator queue unless auto-flagged. It goes to exactly two people. Say this in one sentence on the screen where the guest makes the choice, because the entire value of the feature is that they believe it.

Retention, in plain language

Originals kept for the tier's archive period, then a warning email, then a downgrade to web resolution — never silent deletion of someone's wedding. Guest contact details are used for the reveal and, absent a checkbox they actually saw, for nothing else. Publish the retention period in the couple's dashboard rather than burying it in terms nobody reads.

10

The reveal, beat by beat

The peak

This is the moment the product is sold on. Build it as a single directed sequence — closer to a title sequence than a page load — and don't make it skippable on the first view.

Before

The waiting room

From 02:00 to 10:00 the link doesn't 404 and it doesn't show a gallery. It shows a dark screen, a soft chemical shimmer, and a countdown: מפתחים. Anyone who opens it early — and hungover guests will, at 06:00 — gets an object rather than a locked door. Add the counts, climbing slowly: frames, voices, secrets.

Beat 1

Arrival

The message lands at 10:00 — not "your album is ready", which is a Dropbox sentence. החתונה שלכם פותחה 🎮 for the couple, החתונה של מייה ודניאל פותחה for guests. SMS in Israel; email as fallback.

Beat 2

The numbers, one at a time

Not a stats grid — a sequence. Each figure counts up, holds, dissolves into the next. 1,847 frames, 143 photographers, 186 secret memories, 73 voices, 46 challenges. Twelve seconds total. Let it breathe.

Beat 3

One photo

Before any grid: a single full-bleed frame, developing on screen — the most-reacted photo of the night. Nothing else on the page. This is the whole product in one image and it earns three seconds of silence.

Beat 4

A voice

Then it plays one voice note. Someone's friend, ten seconds, slightly too loud, music in the background. This is the moment that converts a nice product into one people tell their friends about, and it costs nothing to build.

Beat 5

The night, in order

A vertical scroll through the evening in chapters keyed to the schedule they entered at setup — לפני, החופה, הארוחה, הריקודים, אחרי חצות. Chronology is the correct structure: it's how they experienced it, and it's what makes 1,800 photos legible.

Beat 6

The awards

Seven, revealed one per screen with the photographer's name. Because it's the next morning, this reads as a warm summary rather than a competition.

Beat 7

Then, and only then, everything

The full grid, the search, the downloads, the secret memories. All the utility, after all the emotion. Reverse this order and the sequence becomes a loading screen in front of a file browser.

The guest reveal is a different edit of the same night and it matters more than it sounds. A guest doesn't get the couple's story — they get the wedding's headline number, the three best photos of the night, their own roll, developed, the photos they appear in, and any award they won. Present their roll as a contact sheet with their name on it. It's a personal object, and personal objects get shared.

One restraint

No music autoplay on the couple's reveal. It will fire in a quiet room, on a phone at full volume, at an unpredictable moment. Offer it as a play button on the first screen and let it run once they've chosen. The voice note in Beat 4 should be the first sound they hear, and it should require a tap.

Waiting room, the directed sequence, chronological chapters, guest edition, per-guest roll as a contact sheet, "photos you're in" via table and tagging.

In

The seven-step setup, the Camera Pass print kit with MC script, a glanceable live view with one mission button, and the post-wedding memory dashboard with full-resolution download.

Six, not fourteen. Do six extremely well: Disposable 2000s, Warm Kodak, B&W Elegant, Polaroid, Tel Aviv Night, Cinematic. Applied at display, reversible after the fact.

Three tiers, one-time, Israeli cards. Free to build, pay to activate.

Out — deliberately

Not in v1

AI recap and semantic search. Face-based albums. Mission voting brackets. Leaderboard. Anniversary capsule. Print products. Guest video. Comments. Native apps. Any language beyond Hebrew and English.

The two things that will actually take the time

The camera. Not the UI — the reliability. On-device compression, an IndexedDB queue that survives a tab kill, retry with backoff, honest state when 340 people share one overloaded cell, and iOS Safari's particular opinions about getUserMedia. Budget more here than feels reasonable; this single component decides whether the product works at all.

The reveal sequence. It's what the product is sold on, and it's a directed animation — a different discipline from the rest of the app. Build it last, but budget it properly rather than letting it decay into a stats page with a fade-in.

12

After the MVP

Roadmap

Wave 1 — after roughly fifteen real weddings

  • AI recap and smart search. The categories couples actually ask for — us together, the parents, people crying, the dancefloor, group photos, late-night chaos — plus a 60-second recap cut to a track they choose. Only worth building once you've seen what a real wedding's photo set looks like.
  • Print products. Lead with the contact sheet poster: every frame from the night as thumbnails on one large print with real edge markings. Beautiful, cheap to produce, perfectly on-brand, and no competitor has it. Then the photo book and the print packs.
  • The photographer lane. A pro upload link that drops twenty sneak-peeks into the Darkroom at 01:00. Converts your most dangerous critic into your best channel.
  • Live mission voting. Now that you know how many guests are online simultaneously at a real wedding, decide whether the bracket is viable.

Wave 2 — the year-two product

  • Personal guest albums with opt-in face grouping. The highest-value feature in this document and the one with the most legal surface. Ship it when you can do consent properly.
  • The anniversary capsule. Hold back a set of photos and voice notes; unlock them one year later. Retention, a second emotional peak, and a reason the archive is worth paying for.
  • Beyond weddings. The mechanic isn't wedding-specific — bar mitzvahs, birthdays, army reunions, company events, festivals. Israel has a very high density of large private events, and a bar mitzvah is the same product with different copy. Don't dilute the brand until weddings work, then expand deliberately.
  • Venue and supplier portal. Once venues are referring, give them their own account, referral tracking, and a co-branded pass.

Things to deliberately never build

Unlimited photos at any price. Comments. A social graph. Follows. Cross-wedding feeds. Public weddings. A native app. Each makes the product bigger and worse, and the moment you add unlimited uploads you're competing with six funded companies on their terms instead of yours.

The signature

Brand

A product gets one sentence in someone's memory. This one should get: the wedding where you only had fifteen photos.

The Roll

Fifteen shots. No do-overs. They develop in the morning. Not the album, not the AI, not the challenges — the constraint and the delay, together. That's the sentence a guest repeats at the next wedding, and it's the thing no competitor can copy without abandoning their own positioning, because every one of them sells "capture everything".

The constraint is also the product's honesty. Fifteen photos taken carefully by someone who loves you is a better artifact than four hundred taken carelessly, and everyone knows it the moment it's said out loud.

The visual signature: the edge rebate

Every brand needs a mark that appears where the product does. Yours is the film edge marking — the tiny orange type printed along the border of a negative. SHOTLY 400 · MAYA & DANIEL · 23. It goes on every exported photo, every share card, the Camera Pass, the contact sheet poster, and along the top of the venue screen. It's authentic to the medium rather than pasted on top of it, so nobody experiences it as a watermark and nobody crops it out.

The physical signature: the Camera Pass

The card on the table is the most-seen object the brand owns — 350 people per wedding, at eye level, for five hours. Design it as an actual object: the format of a film box, the shot count in large type, the challenge line, the QR, and the edge rebate along the bottom. When another supplier picks one up, it should read as a designed product, not a printed instruction.

Say

Money

Pricing

One-time per wedding, never a subscription. Nobody wants a recurring charge attached to their wedding, and a couple who cancels in month four leaves you holding their memories.

The anchor that matters in Israel

Don't price against software. Price against the magnet photographer — a standard Israeli wedding line item at roughly ₪2,500–4,000 for a booth producing fridge magnets. Next to that, ₪790 for a camera in every guest's hand producing 1,800 photos, voice notes and an album is an obvious yes. The wider frame — a ₪10,000 photographer inside a ₪120,000 wedding — means this is a rounding error the couple decides on emotionally, in about four minutes.

Gate on guest count first, features second. Guest count is the honest cost driver, it's a number couples already know, and it scales price with the size of the wedding automatically.

Structural decisions

  • Free to build, pay to activate. The entire setup, the style picker, the pass preview, the guest camera demo — all free. Payment gates the QR going live. By then the couple has designed their wedding's camera.
  • Never charge guests. Never sell extra film. The instant a guest sees a payment screen at a wedding, the product is over. Extra film is earned, not bought — and that boundary is part of the brand.
  • Late upgrade is the best revenue line you have. A couple two days before the wedding, excited, has very high willingness to upgrade. Make it one tap from the live view. Also allow upgrading after the wedding — someone who just watched their reveal will buy the AI recap and the permanent archive on the spot.
  • Supplier wholesale. Photographers, venues and planners buy at ₪500 and resell at ₪1,200–1,500 as an add-on. Their margin buys you distribution you can't otherwise afford.

15

What could kill it

Risk

In rough order of how likely each one is to actually happen.

Risk · very likely

Nobody scans

The product is empty and the couple feels cheated. Fix: the MC script is a shipped deliverable, not a suggestion. Passes at seven locations, not one. Pre-seed the Darkroom with the getting-ready roll. Measure scan rate per wedding and treat anything under 25% as a product failure you own, not a customer failure.

Risk · very likely

The network dies at 23:00

340 phones, one cell, one hall. Fix: offline-first from day one. Compress on device to ~1.5MB, queue in IndexedDB, upload with backoff, survive tab kills, and show honest state — 3 פריימים ממתינים לשליחה. The shot must be captured and counted instantly regardless of the network; delivery is a background problem.

Risk · likely

Web camera photos look worse

getUserMedia gives less than the native camera app. Fix: accept it and own it — imperfection is the pitch, and the alternative (a native camera picker) reintroduces previews, retakes and camera-roll leakage. Recover quality where it's free: request the highest resolution the device offers, force flash where supported, and make the style do real work in low light.

Risk · likely

It reads as tacky

"An app that makes guests stare at phones at a wedding" is a real objection from parents, rabbis and photographers. Fix: Ceremony Mode, no leaderboard by default, no comments, and a shot limit that structurally reduces phone time. The limit is the answer to the objection — argue it, don't hide from it.

Risk · possible

Something bad on the projector

One explicit or cruel photo on a three-metre screen ends the relationship and the referral. Fix: automatic screening, a 90-second hold buffer, Best Man Mode, one-tap kill, and a physical rule — the screen is never in direct view of the chuppah.

Risk · possible

The couple is disappointed the next morning

1,800 blurry dark photos is not automatically a gift. Fix: the reveal must be edited. Never open on the full grid. Curation — awards, chapters, the single best frame, the voices — is what turns a pile of files into a memory, and it's the whole reason the AI recap is worth building in wave 1.

Risk · structural

Seasonality and one-shot customers

Israeli weddings cluster hard, and every customer buys exactly once. Fix: supplier channel for baseline volume, the guest-to-couple loop for compounding, print products for post-event revenue, and eventual expansion to bar mitzvahs and birthdays — which conveniently fill the winter.

Risk · structural

A funded competitor copies the mechanic

The shot limit is one afternoon of work to clone. Fix: the moat isn't the feature, it's the combination plus the things that don't live in the codebase — the print kit, the supplier relationships, the Hebrew challenge writing, the reveal direction, the object on the table. And note that the incumbents can't adopt a shot limit without contradicting their own marketing.

Things that weren't in the brief

Additions
The most valuable one

Ceremony Mode

The camera locks during the chuppah. Full screen, no viewfinder, one line: שימו את הטלפונים. זה הרגע שלהם. It unlocks automatically when the ceremony ends. This inverts the product's biggest objection — instead of encouraging phones it manages them — and it's the single feature that turns photographers, venues and mothers-in-law into advocates. One timer, one screen state.

Turns rival into channel

The photographer's frame

A pro upload link. At 01:00 the photographer drops twenty sneak-peeks into the Darkroom and they land while the party is still going. The couple gets professional images the same night, the photographer gets a delivery channel and credit on the projector, and you get a supplier who sells you.

Emotional

The couple's own roll

Give the couple fifteen exposures too. They'll shoot four, all at moments nobody else could reach, and it will be the section of the album they open first. Present it separately: הרול שלכם.

Content

The roll opens early

Two hours before the ceremony, for the wedding party. Getting ready, the parents arriving, the last nervous minutes. Some of the best photographs of any wedding, a window most photographers bill extra for, and it solves the cold-start problem for free.

Retention

The anniversary capsule

Hold back a set of photos and voice notes at the reveal. Unlock them one year later. A second emotional peak, a reason the archive is worth paying for, and a message you get to send a couple on their first anniversary.

Reach

For the people who couldn't come

A link the couple sends to the fifty guests who weren't there. Grandparents abroad, a friend with a newborn. Emotionally strong, and it puts the product in front of people who didn't attend at zero cost.

Placement

The guestbook lives at the end of the roll

Don't build a separate guestbook section nobody visits. Put the prompt at the exact moment the last frame is spent — the most sentimental point of the guest's entire evening. Conversion will be several times higher than any menu item.

Positioning

The no-comments rule, stated out loud

Make the absence of comments a public product principle. "Reactions only. Nobody gets to write anything under a photo at your wedding." It's a differentiator, it's a trust signal, and it costs nothing.

Craft

Sound design as a feature

The shutter, the film advance, the wind at the end of the roll, the soft tone when something develops. Four sound files decide whether the camera feels like a toy or an object. Budget real time for them, and let guests mute in one tap.

Local

Built for Israeli weddings specifically

RTL throughout, not retrofitted. 400-guest scale. Challenges written in Hebrew by someone who's been to one. ₪ pricing anchored on the magnet photographer. Tuesday and Thursday nights, ceremonies at 20:30, dancing until 02:00. This is a category where being obviously local beats being obviously international.

Three engineering notes that follow directly from the design

  • Capture and delivery are separate. The shutter writes to IndexedDB and decrements the counter synchronously; upload is a background worker with retry. A guest must never see a spinner after pressing the button, and must never lose a frame to a dead network.
  • Styles are LUTs, applied twice. A WebGL or canvas LUT pass at display time for the web, and a server-side bake at export. Same LUT file in both places. This is what makes styles reversible and keeps the originals clean.
  • No realtime infrastructure in v1. The Darkroom polls; a 25-minute develop delay makes 10-second polling more than adequate. Only live missions need anything faster, and they can poll at 2s for their five-minute window. Don't buy a websocket layer to serve a feature that's deliberately slow.
